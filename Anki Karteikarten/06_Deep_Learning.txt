What does a single artificial neuron compute?	A weighted sum of its inputs plus a bias, passed through an activation function. The weighted sum is the pre-activation and the result is the post-activation.<br><br>\[ z=\sum_j w_jx_j+b,\qquad a=\sigma(z) \]	DL basics neuron
What is empirical risk?	The average loss over the training data. Minimizing it is the basic training objective for a neural network.<br><br>\[ \mathcal{R}_{\text{emp}}(\theta)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}L\big(y_i,\ f_\theta(x_i)\big) \]	DL basics risk
What does a single neuron with L2 loss and identity activation reduce to?	Ordinary linear regression. It gives exactly the same solution as the normal equations.	DL basics linear-model
Why can the XOR problem not be solved by a linear model?	Because the two classes are not linearly separable: no single hyperplane can separate them. A hidden layer is required to create a non-linear transformation.	DL basics xor
What is the smallest network that solves XOR?	One hidden layer with two ReLU neurons plus a threshold output. The hidden layer remaps the inputs into a space where the classes become linearly separable.	DL basics xor
What is a multi-layer feedforward network?	A composition of alternating affine transformations and non-linear activation functions. Each layer takes the previous layer's output as its input.<br><br>\[ h^{(l)}=\sigma\big(W^{(l)}h^{(l-1)}+b^{(l)}\big) \]	DL architecture feedforward
Why are activation functions essential in a deep network?	Without them the composition of linear layers collapses into a single matrix product, so the whole network would be equivalent to one linear layer.	DL architecture activation
What does the universal approximation theorem state?	A network with a single hidden layer and a continuous, bounded, non-constant activation can approximate any continuous function on a compact set to arbitrary accuracy, given enough hidden units.	DL theory universal-approximation
What is the practical limitation of the universal approximation theorem?	It guarantees existence but says nothing about how many neurons are needed or whether training will find those weights. The required width may be astronomically large.	DL theory universal-approximation
Why are deep networks preferred over one very wide layer?	Depth achieves the same approximation quality far more parameter-efficiently, because each layer can reuse and compose features built by earlier layers.	DL architecture depth
Why is restricting the model space useful despite universal approximation?	Because an unrestricted model space leads to overfitting. Limiting capacity is a form of regularization.	DL regularization capacity
What are the two main problems with deep feedforward networks?	Vanishing gradients when using saturating activations across many layers, and being outperformed by methods like SVMs on small datasets.	DL training problems
What is the softmax function and what is it used for?	It exponentiates each output and normalizes by the sum of all exponentials, producing a probability distribution over classes. It is the standard output layer for multi-class classification.<br><br>\[ \mathrm{softmax}(z)_k=\frac{e^{z_k}}{\sum_{j}e^{z_j}} \]	DL activation softmax
Why is softmax used instead of argmax at the output?	Because argmax is not differentiable, so no gradient could flow. Softmax is a smooth, differentiable approximation.	DL activation softmax
What is the chain rule for computational graphs?	The derivative along a path is the product of the local derivatives, and when multiple paths connect two nodes their contributions are summed.<br><br>\[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial x}=\sum_{\text{paths}}\ \prod_{\text{edges}}\frac{\partial\,\text{out}}{\partial\,\text{in}} \]	DL backpropagation chain-rule
What is backpropagation in one sentence?	Data flows forward through the network to compute the loss, and the loss gradient flows backwards through the same graph reusing intermediate results.	DL backpropagation
What is the error signal delta in backpropagation?	The derivative of the loss with respect to a neuron's pre-activation. It answers how much the loss changes if that neuron's input sum changes.<br><br>\[ \delta_j^{(l)}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial z_j^{(l)}} \]	DL backpropagation
What is the recursive formula for the error signal?	The delta of a neuron is the weighted sum of the deltas of the neurons it feeds into, multiplied by the derivative of its own activation function.<br><br>\[ \delta^{(l)}=\Big(W^{(l+1)\top}\delta^{(l+1)}\Big)\odot\sigma'\big(z^{(l)}\big) \]	DL backpropagation
How is the gradient for a weight obtained from the error signal?	Multiply the error signal of the target neuron by the output of the source neuron. The gradient for a bias is just the error signal itself.<br><br>\[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_{jk}^{(l)}}=\delta_j^{(l)}\,a_k^{(l-1)},\qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial b_j^{(l)}}=\delta_j^{(l)} \]	DL backpropagation
Why does backpropagation reuse the forward pass results?	Because the local derivatives depend on the activations computed during the forward pass. Caching them avoids recomputing the network for every parameter.	DL backpropagation efficiency
What is gradient descent for neural networks?	Repeatedly move the parameters in the direction opposite to the gradient of the empirical risk, scaled by a learning rate.<br><br>\[ \theta^{(t+1)}=\theta^{(t)}-\alpha\,\nabla_\theta\mathcal{R}\big(\theta^{(t)}\big) \]	DL optimization gradient-descent
What is stochastic gradient descent?	Gradient descent computed on mini-batches instead of the full dataset. Each step is a noisy but unbiased estimate of the true gradient.<br><br>\[ \theta^{(t+1)}=\theta^{(t)}-\alpha\,\frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{i\in B}\nabla_\theta L_i\big(\theta^{(t)}\big) \]	DL optimization sgd
What are the advantages of SGD over full-batch gradient descent?	It is far faster per update, and the gradient noise can help escape poor local minima and saddle points.	DL optimization sgd
What is an epoch?	One complete pass over all mini-batches of the training set, so every training example has been used exactly once.	DL training terminology
What is the effect of batch size on training?	A large batch gives stable training and fast convergence but risks poor minima and uses a lot of memory; a small batch is memory-friendly and adds regularizing noise but trains slowly and unstably.	DL training batch-size
Why is a small batch size a form of regularization?	Because the gradient noise it introduces prevents the optimizer from settling into sharp minima that generalize poorly.	DL training regularization
What is the condition number of the Hessian and why does it matter?	The ratio of largest to smallest eigenvalue. A large value means very uneven curvature, so no single learning rate works well in all directions.<br><br>\[ \kappa=\frac{\lambda_{\max}}{\lambda_{\min}} \]	DL optimization conditioning
Why does an ill-conditioned loss surface hurt gradient descent?	Steps must be small enough for the high-curvature direction, which makes progress in the low-curvature direction extremely slow.	DL optimization conditioning
What kind of critical points dominate in neural network loss surfaces?	Saddle points, not local minima. In high dimensions it is very unlikely that all Hessian eigenvalues share the same sign.	DL optimization loss-landscape
Why are second-order methods problematic for neural networks?	They are attracted to saddle points, which are precisely the critical points that dominate high-dimensional neural loss surfaces.	DL optimization second-order
What is gradient clipping and why is it used?	Rescaling the gradient when its norm exceeds a threshold. It prevents huge destructive updates when the loss surface has cliffs.	DL optimization gradient-clipping
Why do neural network loss surfaces have many symmetries?	Because permuting hidden neurons, flipping signs with odd activations, or rescaling weights between layers can leave the function unchanged while changing the parameters.	DL theory symmetry
What are the three types of weight-space symmetry?	Sign-flip symmetry (using odd activations like tanh), scale symmetry (multiply in one layer and divide in the next), and permutation symmetry (swapping hidden neurons).	DL theory symmetry
What is momentum in optimization?	An exponentially decaying moving average of past gradients used as the update direction. It accumulates consistent directions and damps oscillations.<br><br>\[ v^{(t)}=\gamma v^{(t-1)}+\alpha\,\nabla_\theta L,\qquad \theta^{(t+1)}=\theta^{(t)}-v^{(t)} \]	DL optimization momentum
Why does momentum help escape saddle points?	Because accumulated velocity carries the parameters through flat regions where the current gradient is nearly zero.	DL optimization momentum
What is Nesterov momentum?	Momentum with a lookahead correction: the gradient is evaluated at the position the momentum would take you to, rather than at the current position.<br><br>\[ v^{(t)}=\gamma v^{(t-1)}+\alpha\,\nabla_\theta L\big(\theta^{(t)}-\gamma v^{(t-1)}\big) \]	DL optimization momentum
What is a learning rate schedule?	A rule for decreasing the learning rate over training, typically linearly to a final value. Large early steps explore, small later steps refine.	DL optimization learning-rate
What are cyclical learning rates for?	Periodically raising the learning rate again to escape a current basin and explore other regions of the loss surface.	DL optimization learning-rate
What is AdaGrad?	An optimizer that gives each parameter its own learning rate by dividing by the square root of the accumulated sum of its squared past gradients.<br><br>\[ g^{(t)}=\sum_{s\le t}\big(\nabla_\theta L\big)^2,\qquad \theta^{(t+1)}=\theta^{(t)}-\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{g^{(t)}}+\epsilon}\nabla_\theta L \]	DL optimization adagrad
What is the drawback of AdaGrad?	The accumulated squared gradients only ever grow, so the effective learning rate shrinks monotonically and training can stall prematurely.	DL optimization adagrad
What is RMSProp and how does it fix AdaGrad?	It replaces the running sum of squared gradients with an exponentially decaying moving average, so old gradients are forgotten and the learning rate does not vanish.<br><br>\[ g^{(t)}=\rho\,g^{(t-1)}+(1-\rho)\big(\nabla_\theta L\big)^2 \]	DL optimization rmsprop
What is Adam?	RMSProp combined with momentum: it keeps exponentially decaying averages of both the gradient and the squared gradient, with bias correction for both.<br><br>\[ m^{(t)}=\beta_1m^{(t-1)}+(1-\beta_1)g,\qquad v^{(t)}=\beta_2v^{(t-1)}+(1-\beta_2)g^2 \]	DL optimization adam
Why does Adam need bias correction?	Because both moving averages are initialized at zero, which biases them toward zero in the early steps. Dividing by one minus the decay rate to the power t removes this bias.<br><br>\[ \hat m=\frac{m^{(t)}}{1-\beta_1^{\,t}},\qquad \hat v=\frac{v^{(t)}}{1-\beta_2^{\,t}},\qquad \theta^{(t+1)}=\theta^{(t)}-\frac{\alpha\,\hat m}{\sqrt{\hat v}+\epsilon} \]	DL optimization adam
What is regularization in one sentence?	Anything that reduces test error, possibly at the cost of increased training error.	DL regularization
What is L2 regularization (weight decay)?	Adding the squared norm of the parameters times a penalty factor to the loss. Its gradient adds a term proportional to the weights, pulling them toward zero.<br><br>\[ \tilde{\mathcal{R}}(\theta)=\mathcal{R}(\theta)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\theta\|_2^2 \]	DL regularization l2
What is the geometric effect of L2 regularization?	It shrinks the solution along the low-curvature directions of the Hessian much more than along high-curvature ones. Directions that barely matter for the loss get shrunk most.	DL regularization l2
How does the L2-regularized solution relate to the unregularized one in eigen-coordinates?	Each eigen-direction of the Hessian is rescaled by its eigenvalue divided by that eigenvalue plus the penalty. Large eigenvalues are almost unaffected, small ones are heavily shrunk.<br><br>\[ \tilde\theta_i=\frac{\lambda_i}{\lambda_i+\lambda}\,\hat\theta_i \]	DL regularization l2
What is ridge regression as a special case?	L2-regularized ordinary least squares. The penalty adds a multiple of the identity to the cross-product matrix before inverting.<br><br>\[ \hat\beta_{\text{ridge}}=\big(X^{\top}X+\lambda I\big)^{-1}X^{\top}y \]	DL regularization ridge
How is the regularization strength chosen in practice?	By cross-validation, evaluating held-out performance across a range of candidate penalty values.	DL regularization hyperparameters
What is structural risk minimization?	Choosing the smallest hypothesis space such that enlarging it further does not improve performance, for example selecting the polynomial degree.	DL theory model-selection
What is early stopping?	Monitoring validation error during training and stopping when it starts to rise, then keeping the parameters from that point.	DL regularization early-stopping
Why does early stopping act like a regularizer?	Because limiting the number of steps limits how far the parameters can move from their small initialization, which is effectively a constraint on their magnitude.	DL regularization early-stopping
What determines a good patience value for early stopping?	The smoothness of the validation loss. A noisy loss curve requires more patience to avoid stopping on a random fluctuation.	DL regularization early-stopping
What is an ensemble and why does it work?	Combining several models and averaging their predictions. It works because different models make different errors, which partially cancel out.	DL regularization ensembles
What is dropout?	Randomly setting a fraction of activations to zero during training, with a different random mask for each mini-batch.	DL regularization dropout
Why does dropout prevent overfitting?	It prevents complex co-adaptations between neurons, forcing each unit to be useful on its own rather than relying on specific partners.	DL regularization dropout
Why must activations be rescaled when using dropout?	To keep the expected value of the activations unchanged between training and inference. The surviving activations are scaled by one over one minus the drop probability.<br><br>\[ a_{\text{train}}=\frac{a\odot m}{1-p},\qquad m_j\sim\mathrm{Bern}(1-p) \]	DL regularization dropout
What is data augmentation and why is it regularization?	Generating additional training examples through label-preserving transformations. It increases data variety and trains the model over a broader input range.	DL regularization augmentation
What is batch normalization?	Normalizing the activations of a hidden layer using the mean and variance computed over the current mini-batch, then rescaling with two learned parameters per channel.<br><br>\[ \hat a=\frac{a-\mu_B}{\sqrt{\sigma_B^2+\epsilon}},\qquad y=\gamma\hat a+\beta \]	DL normalization batchnorm
Why was batch normalization introduced?	Because weights in earlier layers keep changing during training, so later layers constantly face shifting input distributions. Normalizing per mini-batch stabilizes those inputs.	DL normalization batchnorm
Why does batch normalization use mini-batch statistics rather than full-dataset statistics?	For memory and computational efficiency: the statistics must be recomputed at every step, and using the whole dataset each time would be prohibitive.	DL normalization batchnorm
Why does batch normalization include learnable scale and shift parameters?	So the network can undo the normalization if that is beneficial. It learns how much normalization to actually apply.	DL normalization batchnorm
What is the typical layer ordering with batch normalization?	Fully connected layer, then batch normalization, then the activation function.	DL normalization batchnorm
What is the effect of batch normalization on the loss landscape?	It makes the loss surface smoother, which permits larger learning rates and faster, more stable training.	DL normalization batchnorm
Why must weights be initialized randomly rather than at zero?	To break symmetry. Identical weights produce identical gradients, so all neurons in a layer would remain identical forever.	DL initialization
What is Glorot (Xavier) initialization?	Sampling weights uniformly with a range depending on the sum of input and output dimensions. It keeps the activation variance roughly constant across layers.<br><br>\[ W\sim\mathcal{U}\!\left(-\sqrt{\tfrac{6}{n_{in}+n_{out}}},\ \sqrt{\tfrac{6}{n_{in}+n_{out}}}\right) \]	DL initialization glorot
What is He initialization and when is it used?	A Gaussian initialization with variance two over the number of inputs, designed for ReLU layers where half the activations are zeroed out.<br><br>\[ W\sim N\!\left(0,\ \frac{2}{n_{in}}\right) \]	DL initialization he
What is the vanishing gradient problem?	With saturating activations, each local derivative is less than 1, so the product across many layers shrinks toward zero and early layers barely update.	DL training vanishing-gradients
What is a saturating neuron?	A neuron whose activation is in the flat region of a sigmoid or tanh, where the derivative is nearly zero, so it stops receiving gradient updates.	DL activation saturation
What is the ReLU activation and what are its advantages?	The maximum of zero and the input. For positive inputs the gradient is exactly 1, so there is no vanishing gradient, and it is very cheap to compute.<br><br>\[ \mathrm{ReLU}(z)=\max(0,z),\qquad \mathrm{ReLU}'(z)=\mathbf{1}\{z\gt 0\} \]	DL activation relu
What is the dying ReLU problem?	If a neuron's pre-activation becomes negative for all training points, its gradient is permanently zero and the neuron never updates again.	DL activation relu
What is Leaky ReLU?	A ReLU variant with a small positive slope for negative inputs. The non-zero gradient prevents neurons from dying permanently.<br><br>\[ f(z)=\begin{cases}z,&amp; z\gt 0\\ \alpha z,&amp; z\le 0\end{cases} \]	DL activation relu
What is the Exponential Linear Unit (ELU)?	An activation that is the identity for positive inputs and a scaled exponential minus 1 for negative ones. It provides smooth negative saturation with non-zero gradients.<br><br>\[ f(z)=\begin{cases}z,&amp; z\gt 0\\ \alpha\big(e^{z}-1\big),&amp; z\le 0\end{cases} \]	DL activation elu
Which output activation goes with which problem type?	Identity for unrestricted real outputs, sigmoid for a single probability, softmax for multi-class probabilities, and exponential for strictly positive outputs.	DL architecture output-layer
Why is identity output with MSE loss equivalent to maximizing a Gaussian likelihood?	Because the log-likelihood of a Gaussian with fixed variance contains exactly the negative squared error term, so minimizing MSE maximizes the Gaussian likelihood.	DL loss-functions mse
What is the binary cross-entropy loss?	The negative log-likelihood of a Bernoulli distribution: minus the log of the predicted probability of the true class. It pairs with a sigmoid output.<br><br>\[ L=-\big[y\log\hat p+(1-y)\log(1-\hat p)\big] \]	DL loss-functions cross-entropy
What is categorical cross-entropy?	The negative log of the predicted probability assigned to the true class, used with a softmax output over multiple classes.<br><br>\[ L=-\sum_{k=1}^{K}y_k\log\hat p_k \]	DL loss-functions cross-entropy
Why is combining the sigmoid with cross-entropy numerically favourable?	The composition simplifies algebraically so that the loss can be written directly in terms of the pre-activation, avoiding the numerically unstable exponentials.	DL loss-functions numerical-stability
What is the derivative of the cross-entropy loss with respect to the pre-activation for a sigmoid output?	Simply the predicted probability minus the true label. The sigmoid derivative cancels exactly against the loss derivative.<br><br>\[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial z}=\hat p-y \]	DL loss-functions backpropagation
Why can loss and accuracy diverge during training?	Because the loss reacts to changes in confidence while accuracy only reacts to which side of the decision threshold a prediction falls on.	DL evaluation metrics
What is calibration of a classifier?	The property that a predicted probability matches the observed frequency, so among predictions of 0.6 about 60 percent should actually be positive.	DL evaluation calibration
What is the core idea of a convolutional neural network?	Automatic feature extraction: learned filters slide over the input, so features can be detected anywhere without learning separate weights for each position.	DL cnn
What is the output of a convolutional filter at one position?	The sum of the elementwise products of the filter weights with the covered input values, plus a bias.<br><br>\[ (f*x)_{ij}=\sum_{m}\sum_{n}w_{mn}\,x_{i+m,\,j+n}+b \]	DL cnn convolution
What is sparse interaction in a CNN?	Each output depends only on a small local region of the input rather than the whole image. This drastically reduces the number of parameters.	DL cnn properties
What is parameter sharing in a CNN?	The same filter weights are reused at every spatial position, so one feature detector applies across the whole image.	DL cnn properties
What is translation equivariance and why does it matter?	Shifting the input shifts the feature map by the same amount, so a feature is detected regardless of where it appears in the image.	DL cnn properties
How many channels does a filter always have?	The same number as its input. A 5-by-5 filter on a colour image is really 5 by 5 by 3.	DL cnn dimensions
How many parameters does a convolutional layer have?	The kernel height times width times input channels, plus one bias, all multiplied by the number of output channels.<br><br>\[ \#=\big(k_h\cdot k_w\cdot c_{in}+1\big)\cdot c_{out} \]	DL cnn parameters
What is valid padding and what is its effect on size?	No padding at all, so the output shrinks: output size equals input size minus kernel size plus 1.<br><br>\[ o=i-k+1 \]	DL cnn padding
What is same padding?	Adding enough zeros around the border so that the output has the same spatial size as the input.	DL cnn padding
What is the output size formula with padding and stride?	Input size minus kernel size plus twice the padding, all divided by the stride, plus one (rounded down).<br><br>\[ o=\left\lfloor\frac{i-k+2p}{s}\right\rfloor+1 \]	DL cnn dimensions
What is stride in a convolution?	The step size by which the filter moves. A stride of 2 moves two pixels at a time and roughly halves the output dimensions.	DL cnn stride
What is max pooling?	Taking the maximum over each small window, typically 2 by 2 with stride 2. It downsamples the feature map and adds local translation invariance.	DL cnn pooling
When is average pooling preferred over max pooling?	When detail matters and noise robustness is less important, for example in some NLP applications. Max pooling is more noise resistant.	DL cnn pooling
What is 1D convolution used for?	Sequential data such as sensor time series or text, where the filter rolls along a single dimension with depth equal to the alphabet or embedding size.	DL cnn 1d
What is 3D convolution used for?	Volumetric data such as MRI scans or video, where the kernel moves in three spatial or spatio-temporal directions.	DL cnn 3d
What is a dilated convolution?	A convolution whose filter elements are spread apart with gaps. It enlarges the receptive field without adding parameters.	DL cnn dilated
What is the benefit of dilated convolutions?	More context is captured per layer with the same number of weights, giving better runtime and a larger effective receptive field.	DL cnn dilated
What is a transposed convolution?	An upsampling operation that increases spatial dimensions, essentially a convolution with heavy padding. It is used to restore an inner layer to the original size.	DL cnn transposed-convolution
What artefact do transposed convolutions produce and why?	Checkerboard patterns, caused by uneven overlap of the filter across output positions. Plain upsampling avoids this.	DL cnn transposed-convolution
What is a separable convolution?	A convolution decomposed into two smaller sequential operations, for example a vertical filter followed by a horizontal one, with a similar overall effect.	DL cnn separable
What is depthwise separable convolution?	Applying one spatial filter per input channel, then a 1-by-1 convolution to combine the channels. It uses far fewer parameters and computations.	DL cnn separable
What is the trade-off of separable convolutions?	Fewer parameters and faster computation, but not every kernel can be expressed this way, so the model space is limited.	DL cnn separable
What is a 1-by-1 convolution actually doing?	Acting as a fully connected layer across channels at each pixel position, mixing channel information without any spatial extent.	DL cnn network-in-network
What is global average pooling and why is it used?	Averaging each entire feature map to a single number, then connecting those to the output. It removes the parameter-heavy final fully connected layer and acts as a regularizer.	DL cnn pooling
What is the receptive field of a neuron?	The region of the original input that influences that neuron's value. It grows layer by layer with kernel size and stride.	DL cnn receptive-field
How does the receptive field grow across layers?	The receptive field of the previous layer is multiplied by the stride and enlarged by the kernel size minus the stride. Deeper layers see progressively more context.<br><br>\[ r_{l}=r_{l-1}+\big(k_l-1\big)\prod_{j\lt l}s_j \]	DL cnn receptive-field
What is the difference between convolution and cross-correlation?	Convolution flips the kernel before sliding, cross-correlation does not. CNN libraries usually implement cross-correlation while calling it convolution.	DL cnn convolution
Why does it not matter that CNNs use cross-correlation instead of true convolution?	Because the kernel weights are learned anyway, so a flipped kernel is just as learnable as an unflipped one.	DL cnn convolution
What does cross-correlation measure conceptually?	Where two signals are most similar. Its peaks mark positions where the pattern in the filter best matches the input.	DL cnn convolution
What was AlexNet's contribution?	It popularized deep CNNs on ImageNet using two parallel convolution streams and the ReLU activation.	DL cnn architectures
What is the key idea of GoogLeNet?	The inception block, which runs several differently-sized convolution paths in parallel and concatenates their outputs, letting the network choose the useful scales.	DL cnn architectures
What is a residual network (ResNet)?	A network with skip connections that add the input of a block to its output, so the block only has to learn a residual correction rather than a full mapping.	DL cnn architectures resnet
Why do skip connections help training?	They give gradients a direct path back through the network and let the model skip layers that are not useful, making very deep networks trainable.	DL cnn architectures resnet
What is the dimensional requirement for a residual connection?	The block output and the skipped input must have the same shape so they can be added. A projection is needed when the shapes differ.	DL cnn architectures resnet
What is U-Net and what is it used for?	An encoder-decoder architecture with skip connections that upsamples via transposed convolutions back to the input dimension. It is used for semantic segmentation.	DL cnn architectures unet
What is semantic segmentation?	Labelling every individual pixel with the class it belongs to, rather than assigning a single label to the whole image.	DL cnn applications
How does object localization work in a CNN?	Two heads on shared features: a classification head predicting class scores and a regression head predicting bounding box coordinates.	DL cnn applications
What is a recurrent neural network?	A network that processes a sequence one token at a time, carrying a hidden state forward that acts as short-term memory.	DL rnn
What are the three weight matrices of a basic RNN?	Input-to-hidden, hidden-to-hidden (which carries information across time), and hidden-to-output.<br><br>\[ h_t=\tanh\big(Wx_t+Uh_{t-1}+b\big),\qquad y_t=Vh_t \]	DL rnn
Why is the number of RNN weights independent of sequence length?	Because the same weight matrices are reused at every time step. This is what lets an RNN handle variable-length inputs.	DL rnn
What is a bidirectional RNN?	Two recurrent passes, one forward and one backward through the sequence, whose hidden states are combined. Each position then has both past and future context.	DL rnn bidirectional
What is backpropagation through time?	Unrolling the recurrent network across time steps and applying ordinary backpropagation to the unrolled graph, summing gradients over all time steps.	DL rnn training
Why do RNNs suffer from vanishing and exploding gradients?	Because the same weight matrix is multiplied repeatedly across time steps, so its influence decays or explodes geometrically with sequence length.	DL rnn vanishing-gradients
What is the practical consequence of vanishing gradients in RNNs?	Long-term dependencies become essentially impossible to learn, because the gradient signal from distant time steps disappears.	DL rnn vanishing-gradients
What are the standard solutions to exploding gradients?	Gradient clipping, gated architectures like GRU and LSTM, careful weight initialization, and batch normalization.	DL rnn vanishing-gradients
What is the key architectural idea of an LSTM?	A cell state that flows through time with only additive updates, controlled by gates that decide what to forget, add and output.<br><br>\[ c_t=f_t\odot c_{t-1}+i_t\odot\tilde c_t,\qquad h_t=o_t\odot\tanh(c_t) \]	DL rnn lstm
What are the three gates of an LSTM and what do they do?	The forget gate decides what to discard from the cell state, the input gate decides what new information to add, and the output gate decides what to expose as the hidden state.<br><br>\[ f_t=\sigma\big(W_f[h_{t-1},x_t]\big),\quad i_t=\sigma\big(W_i[\cdot]\big),\quad o_t=\sigma\big(W_o[\cdot]\big) \]	DL rnn lstm
Why does the LSTM cell state avoid vanishing gradients?	Because updates are additive rather than multiplicative, so gradients can flow backwards through many time steps without repeated shrinking.	DL rnn lstm
What is a GRU and how does it differ from an LSTM?	A simpler gated unit with only an update gate and a reset gate, and no separate cell state. It is cheaper but often performs comparably.	DL rnn gru
What does the GRU update gate control?	How much of the old hidden state is kept versus how much of the newly proposed state is written in. It interpolates between memory and new information.<br><br>\[ h_t=(1-z_t)\odot h_{t-1}+z_t\odot\tilde h_t \]	DL rnn gru
What does the GRU reset gate control?	How much of the previous hidden state contributes to the candidate new state. Setting it near zero lets the unit forget the past when computing the new proposal.	DL rnn gru
What happens to a GRU if both gates are always zero?	The gradient through the hidden state vanishes completely, so the network cannot learn any temporal dependency at all.	DL rnn gru
What is the fundamental limitation of RNNs including LSTMs and GRUs?	The entire input history has to be compressed into one fixed-size final hidden state, which is not enough for long sequences.	DL rnn limitations
What problem does attention solve?	It lets the decoder access all encoder hidden states rather than only the final one, choosing at each step which parts of the input are relevant.	DL attention
How does an attention mechanism compute its weights?	A small network scores the relevance of each encoder state against the current decoder state, and the scores are normalized into weights that form a weighted sum.	DL attention
What is the fundamental architectural difference between a Transformer and an RNN?	An RNN reads the input sequentially, while a Transformer processes all positions in parallel using only attention, with no recurrence or convolution.	DL transformer
How does a Transformer represent word order without recurrence?	Through positional encodings added to the token embeddings, since attention alone is permutation invariant.	DL transformer positional-encoding
What are the query, key and value in self-attention?	Three different linear projections of the same input. Queries are matched against keys to produce attention weights, which are used to combine the values.	DL transformer self-attention
What is the self-attention formula in words?	Take the scaled dot products of queries with keys, apply softmax to get weights, and use them to form a weighted sum of the values.<br><br>\[ \mathrm{Attention}(Q,K,V)=\mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V \]	DL transformer self-attention
Why is the dot product scaled by the square root of the key dimension?	Because dot products grow with dimension, which would push the softmax into a saturated regime with vanishing gradients. Scaling keeps the variance stable.<br><br>\[ \operatorname{Var}\big(q^{\top}k\big)\propto d_k\ \Rightarrow\ \text{divide by }\sqrt{d_k} \]	DL transformer self-attention
What is masking in the Transformer decoder for?	Preventing each position from attending to future tokens, so the model cannot cheat when predicting the next token.	DL transformer masking
What is multi-head attention?	Running several attention mechanisms in parallel on different learned projections and concatenating the results, so different heads can capture different relationships.<br><br>\[ \mathrm{MHA}=\mathrm{Concat}\big(\mathrm{head}_1,\dots,\mathrm{head}_M\big)W^{O} \]	DL transformer multi-head
What is the memory bottleneck of self-attention?	It requires an attention matrix of size sequence length squared. Flash attention addresses this by processing queries, keys and values in blocks.	DL transformer efficiency
What is unsupervised learning?	Learning structure from unlabelled data, for example clustering, dimensionality reduction, or learning a representation for downstream supervised tasks.	DL unsupervised
What is the manifold hypothesis?	High-dimensional real data actually lies on a much lower-dimensional non-linear subspace, which is locally approximately linear.	DL unsupervised manifold
What is an autoencoder?	A network with an encoder mapping the input to a low-dimensional code and a decoder reconstructing the input from that code. It is trained on reconstruction loss.	DL autoencoder
What is an undercomplete autoencoder?	One whose code dimension is smaller than the input dimension, which forces genuine compression.	DL autoencoder
What happens if the code dimension equals the input dimension?	The autoencoder can simply learn the identity function and no useful representation is extracted. Some form of restriction or regularization is required.	DL autoencoder
What is the relationship between a linear autoencoder and PCA?	A linear autoencoder minimizing squared reconstruction error finds the same subspace as PCA. Minimizing reconstruction error and maximizing projected variance are equivalent.<br><br>\[ \min_{W}\ \|X-XWW^{\top}\|_F^2\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \text{PCA} \]	DL autoencoder pca
Why is a non-linear autoencoder a generalization of PCA?	Because non-linear encoders and decoders can capture curved manifolds that a linear projection cannot represent.	DL autoencoder pca
What is a sparse autoencoder?	One with an L1 penalty on the code, keeping the number of simultaneously active hidden units low. Sparsity acts as the capacity restriction.<br><br>\[ L=\|x-\hat x\|_2^2+\lambda\|z\|_1 \]	DL autoencoder regularized
What is a denoising autoencoder?	One trained to reconstruct the clean input from a deliberately corrupted version. It learns to map noisy points back onto the data manifold.	DL autoencoder denoising
Why is a denoising autoencoder attractive computationally?	It regularizes the representation without requiring any Jacobian or Hessian computation, unlike the contractive variant.	DL autoencoder denoising
What is a contractive autoencoder?	One penalizing the Frobenius norm of the encoder Jacobian, so that similar inputs receive similar encodings. It is more stable but requires derivative computations.<br><br>\[ L=\|x-\hat x\|_2^2+\lambda\big\|J_{\text{enc}}(x)\big\|_F^2 \]	DL autoencoder contractive
What is a variational autoencoder?	An autoencoder that describes each latent attribute as a probability distribution rather than a point, which allows modelling uncertainty and generating new samples.	DL vae
Why is the true posterior over the latent variables intractable in a VAE?	Because computing it requires the marginal likelihood of the data, which is an integral over the whole latent space that cannot be evaluated.	DL vae
What is variational inference in the VAE context?	Approximating the intractable true posterior with a simpler tractable distribution, usually Gaussian, chosen to minimize the KL divergence to the truth.	DL vae variational-inference
What is the reparameterization trick and why is it needed?	Rewriting a sample as the mean plus the standard deviation times independent noise, so randomness enters as an input rather than an operation. Without it no gradient can flow through the sampling step.<br><br>\[ z=\mu_\phi(x)+\sigma_\phi(x)\odot\epsilon,\qquad \epsilon\sim N(0,I) \]	DL vae reparameterization
What is the ELBO?	The evidence lower bound: a tractable lower bound on the log marginal likelihood, consisting of an expected reconstruction term minus a KL divergence to the prior.<br><br>\[ \mathcal{L}=E_{q(z\mid x)}\big[\log p(x\mid z)\big]-\mathrm{KL}\big(q(z\mid x)\,\|\,p(z)\big) \]	DL vae elbo
Why is the ELBO a valid lower bound?	Because Jensen's inequality applied to the concave logarithm moves the expectation inside, which can only decrease the value.	DL vae elbo
What are the two terms of the VAE loss interpreted intuitively?	A reconstruction loss measuring fidelity of the decoded output, and a penalty for the latent distribution diverging from the prior.	DL vae elbo
What is mode collapse in a VAE?	When the KL term dominates and the encoder maps every input to essentially the same average latent distribution, so the latent space carries no information.	DL vae problems
What is KL annealing?	Starting training with the KL term switched off and gradually increasing its weight over epochs. It lets the model first learn to reconstruct before being regularized.	DL vae training
What happens to a VAE if the KL term is removed entirely?	The latent space remains stochastic but loses its structure, so the encodings spread out with high variance and no clean separation.	DL vae
Why is a diagonal prior used in a VAE?	It enforces independent latent variables, encouraging each dimension of the latent code to capture a separate factor of variation.	DL vae
What are practical uses of the VAE latent distribution?	Generating new data by sampling in latent space and decoding, and outlier detection based on how the encoder's distribution behaves.	DL vae applications
What is a convolutional autoencoder?	An autoencoder whose encoder uses convolutional layers for downscaling and whose decoder uses transposed convolutions for upscaling. It is far more parameter-efficient on images.	DL autoencoder cnn
What does weight sharing do to the bias-variance trade-off?	It increases bias, because the model is constrained, but reduces variance, because there are far fewer parameters to estimate.	DL cnn theory
Why does the derivative of a convolution weight sum over all its uses?	Because the same weight appears at every spatial position, so the chain rule contributions from every position must be added together.	DL cnn backpropagation
