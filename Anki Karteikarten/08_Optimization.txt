What does it mean for a function to be smooth?	Its derivative exists and is continuous. Being in class C-k means the k-th derivative exists and is continuous.	Opt calculus
What is the gradient of a multivariate function?	The vector of all first partial derivatives. It points in the direction of steepest increase.<br><br>\[ \nabla f(x)=\Big(\tfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_1},\dots,\tfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_d}\Big)^{\top} \]	Opt calculus gradient
What is a directional derivative?	The rate of change of a function along a given direction, computed as the inner product of the gradient with that direction vector.<br><br>\[ D_vf(x)=\nabla f(x)^{\top}v \]	Opt calculus gradient
Why is the gradient the direction of greatest increase?	Because the directional derivative equals the gradient norm times the direction norm times the cosine of the angle between them, which is maximal when the angle is zero.<br><br>\[ \nabla f(x)^{\top}v=\|\nabla f(x)\|\,\|v\|\cos\theta \]	Opt calculus gradient
What is the relationship between the gradient and level curves?	The gradient is orthogonal to the level curve through that point, because moving along a level curve leaves the function value unchanged so the derivative along it is zero.	Opt calculus gradient
What is the Jacobian and what does its determinant mean?	The matrix of all first partial derivatives of a vector-valued function. Its determinant tells how much the map scales volume locally.	Opt calculus jacobian
What does a non-zero Jacobian determinant imply?	The function is locally invertible in a neighbourhood of that point.	Opt calculus jacobian
What is the Hessian and what does it describe?	The matrix of all second partial derivatives, equivalently the Jacobian of the gradient. It describes the local curvature of the function.<br><br>\[ \nabla^2f(x)=\Big(\tfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_i\partial x_j}\Big)_{i,j} \]	Opt calculus hessian
What does the eigenvector of the largest Hessian eigenvalue represent?	The direction of largest curvature. The corresponding eigenvalue is the curvature magnitude in that direction.	Opt calculus hessian curvature
What is directional curvature?	The quadratic form of the Hessian along a direction vector. It is maximized along the eigenvector of the largest eigenvalue.<br><br>\[ v^{\top}\nabla^2f(x)\,v \]	Opt calculus hessian curvature
What is Lipschitz continuity?	The property that the change in function value is bounded by a constant times the change in input. Slopes are uniformly bounded.<br><br>\[ \|f(x)-f(y)\|\ \le\ L\,\|x-y\| \]	Opt analysis lipschitz
What does a Lipschitz gradient mean?	The gradient itself is Lipschitz continuous, which is equivalent to the eigenvalues of the Hessian being bounded. Curvature cannot change arbitrarily fast.	Opt analysis lipschitz
What is a convex set?	A set in which the straight line segment between any two of its points lies entirely inside the set.	Opt convexity
What is a convex function?	One where the chord connecting any two points on the graph lies on or above the function. Formally, the function of a convex combination is at most the convex combination of the values.<br><br>\[ f\big(\theta x+(1-\theta)y\big)\ \le\ \theta f(x)+(1-\theta)f(y),\qquad \theta\in[0,1] \]	Opt convexity
What is the first-order condition for convexity?	The function lies above all of its tangent planes: the function value at any point is at least the linearization from any other point.<br><br>\[ f(y)\ \ge\ f(x)+\nabla f(x)^{\top}(y-x) \]	Opt convexity
What is the second-order condition for convexity?	The Hessian is positive semi-definite everywhere. This can be checked via the determinants of the leading submatrices.<br><br>\[ \nabla^2f(x)\ \succeq\ 0\qquad\forall x \]	Opt convexity
Which operations preserve convexity?	Non-negative weighted sums, composition of a convex function with a linear map, and the pointwise maximum of convex functions.	Opt convexity
Why is every local optimum of a convex function also global?	Because convexity forbids a valley separated from a lower region by a rise: any such configuration would put a chord below the function.	Opt convexity
How many optima does a strictly convex function have?	At most one. Strict convexity rules out flat regions containing several minimizers.	Opt convexity
Why are all norms convex?	Because the triangle inequality plus absolute homogeneity give exactly the convexity inequality.	Opt convexity norms
Why is A-transpose-A always positive semi-definite?	Because its quadratic form equals the squared Euclidean norm of A times the vector, which cannot be negative.	Opt linear-algebra
What is the Sylvester criterion?	A symmetric matrix is positive definite exactly when all leading principal minors are positive. It is a determinant-based test avoiding eigenvalue computation.	Opt linear-algebra positive-definite
What characterizes an indefinite matrix?	It has eigenvalues of both signs. The corresponding critical point is a saddle point.	Opt linear-algebra
What is Taylor's theorem used for in optimization?	Approximating a function locally by a polynomial. The quadratic approximation is preferred because quadratics are well understood and easy to optimize.	Opt taylor
What do the first- and second-order Taylor approximations match?	The first-order version matches value and slope; the second-order version additionally matches curvature.<br><br>\[ f(x+d)\approx f(x)+\nabla f(x)^{\top}d+\tfrac{1}{2}d^{\top}\nabla^2f(x)\,d \]	Opt taylor
What is the first-order necessary condition for an optimum?	The gradient must vanish. It is necessary but not sufficient, since saddle points also have zero gradient.<br><br>\[ \nabla f(x^*)=0 \]	Opt optimality-conditions
What is a stationary point?	A point where the gradient is zero. It can be a minimum, a maximum, or a saddle point.	Opt optimality-conditions
What is the second-order sufficient condition for a local minimum?	The gradient vanishes and the Hessian is positive definite. Positive curvature in every direction guarantees a strict local minimum.<br><br>\[ \nabla f(x^*)=0\quad\text{and}\quad \nabla^2f(x^*)\succ 0 \]	Opt optimality-conditions
What is a saddle point in terms of the Hessian?	A stationary point where the Hessian is indefinite: the function curves upward in some directions and downward in others.	Opt optimality-conditions saddle-point
When are optima guaranteed to exist?	When the function is continuous and the feasible set is compact, that is, closed and bounded (Weierstrass).	Opt optimality-conditions
What determines convexity of a quadratic function?	The eigenvalues of its Hessian. All non-negative means convex with a global minimum; mixed signs mean an indefinite Hessian and a saddle point.	Opt quadratic-functions
What is the condition number of a quadratic problem?	The ratio of the largest to the smallest eigenvalue of the Hessian. A large value means an ill-conditioned, badly behaved optimization problem.<br><br>\[ \kappa=\frac{\lambda_{\max}\big(\nabla^2f\big)}{\lambda_{\min}\big(\nabla^2f\big)} \]	Opt conditioning
Why is a large condition number bad for gradient descent?	Because a step size safe for the high-curvature direction is far too small for the low-curvature direction, causing slow zig-zagging progress.	Opt conditioning gradient-descent
What is unconstrained optimization?	Optimization where the feasible set is the entire space, so no constraints restrict the solution.	Opt problem-types
What are three classic examples of unconstrained optimization in statistics?	Maximum likelihood estimation for a normal distribution, least squares in linear regression, and empirical risk minimization.	Opt problem-types
What are the four components of a risk minimization problem?	The hypothesis space defining the parameter space, the risk measuring prediction error, an optional regularization penalty, and the optimization procedure itself.	Opt risk-minimization
Is ridge regularization convex and smooth?	It is both convex and smooth. Lasso regularization is convex but not smooth, because the absolute value is not differentiable at zero.	Opt regularization
What is a linear program?	An optimization problem with a linear objective and linear constraints. Its feasible region is a polygon.<br><br>\[ \min_x\ c^{\top}x\quad\text{s.t.}\quad Ax\le b \]	Opt problem-types linear-program
Why does the optimum of a linear program lie at a corner?	Because the objective is linear, so it improves monotonically along any direction. The best value is attained at a vertex or along an edge of the feasible polygon.	Opt linear-program
What is the standard form of a linear program?	Minimize a linear objective subject to equality constraints with non-negative variables. Inequalities become equalities via slack variables and free variables are split into positive and negative parts.<br><br>\[ \min_x\ c^{\top}x\quad\text{s.t.}\quad Ax=b,\ x\ge 0 \]	Opt linear-program
What is a quadratic program?	A problem with a quadratic objective and linear constraints. The optimum can lie in the interior or on the boundary of the feasible region.<br><br>\[ \min_x\ \tfrac{1}{2}x^{\top}Qx+c^{\top}x\quad\text{s.t.}\quad Ax\le b \]	Opt problem-types quadratic-program
What is a convex program?	A problem with a convex objective and convex constraints of any form. It is the broadest class where local optimality still implies global optimality.	Opt problem-types convex-program
How is a constrained problem handled with Lagrange multipliers?	Form the Lagrangian as the objective plus the multiplier times the constraint, then set all partial derivatives, including the one with respect to the multiplier, to zero.<br><br>\[ \mathcal{L}(x,\lambda)=f(x)+\lambda\,g(x),\qquad \nabla_x\mathcal{L}=0,\ \nabla_\lambda\mathcal{L}=0 \]	Opt constrained lagrange
How does the Lagrange approach differ for inequality constraints?	The multiplier must also be checked for non-negativity, since only one direction of the constraint is active.	Opt constrained lagrange
What is the constrained formulation of ridge regression?	Minimize the sum of squared residuals subject to the squared coefficient norm being at most a budget. It is equivalent to the penalized form via a Lagrange multiplier.<br><br>\[ \min_\beta\ \|y-X\beta\|_2^2\quad\text{s.t.}\quad \|\beta\|_2^2\le t \]	Opt constrained ridge
What is the general iterative descent scheme?	Repeatedly find a descent direction, choose a step size that reduces the objective, and update the current point until a stopping criterion is met.	Opt first-order
What is a descent direction?	A direction whose directional derivative is negative, that is, any direction within 90 degrees of the negative gradient.<br><br>\[ \nabla f(x)^{\top}d\ \lt\ 0 \]	Opt first-order
What is gradient descent?	Iterative descent using the negative gradient as the search direction, since that is the direction of steepest local decrease.<br><br>\[ x^{(t+1)}=x^{(t)}-\alpha_t\,\nabla f\big(x^{(t)}\big) \]	Opt first-order gradient-descent
Why does the outcome of gradient descent depend on the starting point?	Because for multimodal functions it converges to whichever basin of attraction the start lies in. Small differences in the start can lead to very different results.	Opt first-order multimodality
What is the trade-off in choosing a fixed step size?	Too small gives slow convergence; too large overshoots the minimum and can diverge.	Opt step-size
What is a line search?	Solving a univariate optimization problem at each step to find the best step size along the chosen direction. It is exact but may cost more than it gains.	Opt step-size line-search
What is the Armijo rule?	A sufficient decrease condition: the step must reduce the function by at least a fixed fraction of what the linearized gradient predicts. It rules out steps that barely improve the objective.<br><br>\[ f(x+\alpha d)\ \le\ f(x)+c_1\,\alpha\,\nabla f(x)^{\top}d,\qquad c_1\in(0,1) \]	Opt step-size armijo
What is backtracking line search?	Start with a large step size and repeatedly shrink it until the Armijo condition is satisfied.	Opt step-size line-search
What is the sufficient curvature condition?	A requirement that the slope at the new point be substantially flatter than at the current point. It prevents steps that are too short.<br><br>\[ \nabla f(x+\alpha d)^{\top}d\ \ge\ c_2\,\nabla f(x)^{\top}d,\qquad c_1\lt c_2\lt 1 \]	Opt step-size wolfe
What are the Wolfe conditions?	The Armijo sufficient decrease condition combined with the sufficient curvature condition. Together they exclude both excessively long and excessively short steps.	Opt step-size wolfe
What is the convergence guarantee for gradient descent on a convex function with Lipschitz gradient?	With a step size at most the reciprocal of the Lipschitz constant, the objective gap shrinks proportionally to one over the iteration count. This is linear convergence.<br><br>\[ f\big(x^{(t)}\big)-f(x^*)\ \le\ \frac{\|x^{(0)}-x^*\|^2}{2\alpha t},\qquad \alpha\le\tfrac{1}{L} \]	Opt convergence gradient-descent
What is the optimal step size for a quadratic problem?	The squared gradient norm divided by the quadratic form of the Hessian applied to the gradient. It is derived by minimizing the quadratic Taylor expansion along the gradient.<br><br>\[ \alpha^*=\frac{\|\nabla f\|^2}{\nabla f^{\top}H\,\nabla f} \]	Opt step-size
How does the optimal step size relate to the curvature direction?	If the gradient aligns with the largest-curvature eigenvector the optimal step is the reciprocal of the largest eigenvalue (small); along the smallest-curvature direction it is the reciprocal of the smallest eigenvalue (large).	Opt step-size curvature
What is the fundamental weakness of gradient descent?	It is unaware of curvature. The gradient tells you the direction of steepest descent but nothing about how far you can safely go.	Opt gradient-descent weaknesses
Why are saddle points a bigger problem than local minima in high dimensions?	Because the ratio of saddle points to local minima grows exponentially with dimension: all Hessian eigenvalues sharing a sign becomes increasingly unlikely.	Opt saddle-point high-dimensional
What is a good stopping criterion for gradient descent?	The squared norm of the gradient falling below a small threshold, indicating an approximately stationary point.	Opt convergence stopping
What is gradient descent with momentum?	Updating with an exponentially smoothed average of past gradients rather than the current gradient alone.<br><br>\[ v^{(t)}=\gamma v^{(t-1)}+\alpha\nabla f\big(x^{(t)}\big),\qquad x^{(t+1)}=x^{(t)}-v^{(t)} \]	Opt momentum
What three weaknesses of plain gradient descent does momentum address?	Zig-zagging in narrow valleys, slow crawling in flat regions, and getting stuck at stationary points.	Opt momentum
What does the momentum decay parameter control?	How strongly past gradients influence the current step. Too high a value can cause the minimum to be overshot.	Opt momentum
What is Nesterov accelerated gradient?	Momentum with the gradient evaluated at the lookahead position rather than the current one, which anticipates and damps overshooting near minima.<br><br>\[ v^{(t)}=\gamma v^{(t-1)}+\alpha\,\nabla f\big(x^{(t)}-\gamma v^{(t-1)}\big) \]	Opt momentum nesterov
What is stochastic gradient descent? (Optimization)	Gradient descent where the full gradient is replaced by the gradient over a randomly drawn mini-batch. It is essential when n is large.<br><br>\[ x^{(t+1)}=x^{(t)}-\alpha_t\,\frac{1}{|B|}\sum_{i\in B}\nabla f_i\big(x^{(t)}\big) \]	Opt sgd
Why is the mini-batch gradient an unbiased estimate of the full gradient?	Because the indices are drawn independently and identically, so the expectation of the mini-batch average equals the full-data average.	Opt sgd
What is the main trade-off of SGD?	Much faster computation per step, but noisier directions so convergence quality can suffer.	Opt sgd
Where is SGD noise most problematic?	Near the optimum, where different mini-batches point in conflicting directions and the iterates wander in a confusion region.	Opt sgd
How is SGD noise quantified?	By the average squared deviation of the individual mini-batch gradients from the full gradient at a point.	Opt sgd
What are the two ways to control SGD convergence?	Batch size and step size. Larger batches reduce noise and decreasing step sizes damp the residual wandering.	Opt sgd
What conditions should an SGD step size schedule satisfy?	The sum of the step sizes must diverge so the iterates can travel far enough, while the sum of squared step sizes must converge so the noise is eventually damped.<br><br>\[ \sum_{t}\alpha_t=\infty,\qquad \sum_t\alpha_t^2\lt\infty \]	Opt sgd step-size
What is the drawback of a step size decaying like one over t?	It shrinks very quickly, so the total distance the iterates can still travel becomes small. A slower decay like one over the square root of t travels further.	Opt sgd step-size
What is AdaGrad? (Optimization)	An adaptive method giving each parameter a step size inversely proportional to the square root of its accumulated squared gradients.<br><br>\[ x^{(t+1)}=x^{(t)}-\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{\sum_{s\le t}g_s^2}+\epsilon}\,g_t \]	Opt adaptive adagrad
What is the flaw of AdaGrad?	The accumulator only grows, so step sizes shrink radically and learning eventually stalls.	Opt adaptive adagrad
What is RMSProp?	AdaGrad with the accumulator replaced by an exponentially decaying moving average, which prevents the step size from shrinking to nothing.<br><br>\[ v^{(t)}=\rho\,v^{(t-1)}+(1-\rho)\,g_t^2,\qquad x^{(t+1)}=x^{(t)}-\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{v^{(t)}}+\epsilon}g_t \]	Opt adaptive rmsprop
What is Adam? (Optimization)	Adaptive moment estimation: RMSProp combined with momentum, tracking exponentially decaying estimates of both the first and second gradient moments with bias correction.<br><br>\[ \hat m=\frac{m^{(t)}}{1-\beta_1^t},\ \ \hat v=\frac{v^{(t)}}{1-\beta_2^t},\qquad x^{(t+1)}=x^{(t)}-\frac{\alpha\hat m}{\sqrt{\hat v}+\epsilon} \]	Opt adaptive adam
Why does Adam need bias correction? (Optimization)	Because the moment accumulators start at zero, which biases early estimates downward. Dividing by one minus the decay rate to the power t removes this.	Opt adaptive adam
In what sense does Adam incorporate curvature?	The second moment estimate acts as a proxy for curvature, scaling steps down in directions with consistently large gradients.	Opt adaptive adam
When is momentum helpful versus harmful?	For deterministic gradient descent momentum accelerates and decay slows down; for SGD with high variance between batches momentum can destabilize and decay performs better.	Opt momentum sgd
What is the Newton-Raphson method for optimization?	Setting the first-order Taylor approximation of the gradient to zero, which gives a step equal to the negative inverse Hessian times the gradient.<br><br>\[ x^{(t+1)}=x^{(t)}-\big[\nabla^2f\big(x^{(t)}\big)\big]^{-1}\nabla f\big(x^{(t)}\big) \]	Opt second-order newton
Why does Newton-Raphson solve a quadratic function in one step?	Because the second-order Taylor approximation is exact for a quadratic, so jumping to the minimum of that approximation lands exactly on the true minimum.	Opt second-order newton
What are the drawbacks of Newton-Raphson?	It has no step size control, its direction is only a descent direction if the Hessian is positive definite, and solving the linear system with the Hessian is expensive.	Opt second-order newton
What is relaxed Newton-Raphson?	Newton's method with a step size, typically chosen by the Armijo rule, rather than always taking the full Newton step.	Opt second-order newton
What is a quasi-Newton method?	A method replacing the true Hessian with a cheaper positive definite approximation that is updated at each iteration.	Opt second-order quasi-newton
What is the secant condition underlying quasi-Newton updates?	The approximation must reproduce the observed change in the gradient over the last step, mimicking how a true Hessian relates gradient changes to position changes.<br><br>\[ B_{t+1}\,s_t=y_t,\qquad s_t=x^{(t+1)}-x^{(t)},\ y_t=\nabla f^{(t+1)}-\nabla f^{(t)} \]	Opt second-order quasi-newton
What is BFGS?	The most widely used quasi-Newton method, updating the Hessian approximation with two rank-one corrections built from the position and gradient differences.	Opt second-order bfgs
Why do quasi-Newton methods guarantee descent directions?	Because the Hessian approximation is kept positive definite by construction, so the resulting direction always has a negative directional derivative.	Opt second-order quasi-newton
What is the Gauss-Newton method? (Optimization)	A method for non-linear least squares that approximates the Hessian using only first derivatives of the residuals, discarding the second-derivative term.<br><br>\[ \big(J^{\top}J\big)\,d=-J^{\top}r,\qquad \nabla^2f\approx J^{\top}J \]	Opt least-squares gauss-newton
What assumption does the Gauss-Newton approximation make?	That the residual curvature term is small, which holds when residuals are small or nearly linear. It is specific to least squares problems.	Opt least-squares gauss-newton
What is the Levenberg-Marquardt algorithm?	Gauss-Newton with a positive diagonal matrix added to the approximate Hessian, which rotates the step toward steepest descent and stabilizes convergence far from the optimum.<br><br>\[ \big(J^{\top}J+\mu D\big)\,d=-J^{\top}r,\qquad \mu\gt 0 \]	Opt least-squares levenberg-marquardt
What is Fisher scoring?	Newton-Raphson with the observed Hessian replaced by its expected value, the Fisher information matrix.<br><br>\[ \theta^{(t+1)}=\theta^{(t)}+\big[I(\theta^{(t)})\big]^{-1}S\big(\theta^{(t)}\big),\qquad I=E\big[-\nabla^2\ell\big] \]	Opt second-order fisher-scoring
What is the advantage of Fisher scoring over Newton-Raphson?	The Fisher information matrix is positive definite by construction, so the direction is always a descent direction, and it is cheaper to compute.	Opt second-order fisher-scoring
When are Newton-Raphson and Fisher scoring identical?	When the observed Hessian does not depend on the response, as in logistic regression with the canonical link. The expected and observed information then coincide.	Opt second-order fisher-scoring
What is the practical rule for choosing an optimizer by dimension?	Use Newton-Raphson if second derivatives are available and dimension is low, quasi-Newton for higher dimensions, and Adam or gradient descent with momentum for very high dimensions or when only first derivatives exist.	Opt method-selection
What is coordinate descent?	Minimizing the objective along one coordinate at a time while holding the others fixed, so each step is a univariate problem.	Opt derivative-free coordinate-descent
When is coordinate descent guaranteed to converge?	When the function is a sum of a convex differentiable part and separable convex terms in the individual coordinates.	Opt derivative-free coordinate-descent
What is the soft threshold rule and where does it come from?	The coordinate-wise lasso update, which shifts the unregularized coefficient toward zero by the penalty amount and sets it to zero if it would cross over. It comes from coordinate descent on the L1 objective.<br><br>\[ \hat\beta_j=\mathrm{sign}\big(z_j\big)\,\max\big(|z_j|-\lambda,\ 0\big) \]	Opt lasso coordinate-descent
What is the Nelder-Mead method?	A derivative-free method maintaining a simplex of d+1 points that is reflected, expanded or contracted based on the function values at its vertices.	Opt derivative-free nelder-mead
What are the pros and cons of Nelder-Mead?	It is robust and needs no gradients, but it is slow and offers no convergence guarantee.	Opt derivative-free nelder-mead
What is golden section search?	A univariate bracketing method that places the new evaluation point using the golden ratio, so the same proportional reduction is achieved at every step regardless of which side survives.<br><br>\[ \varphi=\frac{\sqrt5-1}{2}\approx 0.618 \]	Opt univariate golden-section
What is quadratic interpolation for univariate optimization?	Fitting a parabola through three points and taking its minimum as the new candidate. It is faster than golden section but can degenerate.	Opt univariate
What can go wrong with quadratic interpolation?	The parabola may degenerate into a line, the new point may fall outside the search interval, or the same point may be proposed repeatedly.	Opt univariate
What is Brent's method?	An alternation between quadratic interpolation and golden section search, using interpolation when it is safe and falling back on bracketing otherwise. It guarantees at worst linear convergence.	Opt univariate brent
What is simulated annealing?	A stochastic search that samples a candidate from a neighbourhood, always accepts improvements, and accepts worse candidates with a probability controlled by a temperature.	Opt global simulated-annealing
What is the Metropolis acceptance criterion?	Accept a worse candidate with probability equal to the exponential of the negative objective increase divided by the temperature.<br><br>\[ P_{\text{accept}}=\exp\!\left(-\frac{\Delta f}{T}\right) \]	Opt global simulated-annealing
What role does temperature play in simulated annealing?	It controls the exploration-exploitation balance. High temperature accepts worse solutions readily and explores; lowering it over time forces convergence.	Opt global simulated-annealing
What are the pros and cons of simulated annealing?	It works on discrete problems and can escape local minima, but it offers no guarantee of a good result.	Opt global simulated-annealing
What is the multi-start strategy?	Running a local optimizer from many different starting points, since for multimodal functions the result depends on which basin of attraction the start lies in.	Opt global multi-start
What is a basin of attraction?	The set of starting points from which a given algorithm converges to a particular local optimum.	Opt global multi-start
What are the components of an evolutionary algorithm?	A population of candidate solutions, a fitness function to optimize, and operators that create offspring through recombination and mutation followed by survival selection.	Opt evolutionary
What is the difference between chromosome, gene and phenotype?	The chromosome is the whole candidate solution vector, a gene is one of its components, and the phenotype is the actual realized value being evaluated.	Opt evolutionary terminology
What is the difference between comma and plus selection?	In comma selection survivors are chosen only from the offspring, which requires more offspring than parents; in plus selection they are chosen from offspring and parents together.	Opt evolutionary selection
What is uniform crossover?	Each gene of the offspring is taken from the first parent with a fixed probability and from the second otherwise.	Opt evolutionary recombination
What is intermediate recombination?	The offspring is the componentwise average of the parents rather than a selection between them.<br><br>\[ x_{\text{child}}=\tfrac{1}{2}\big(x_{p_1}+x_{p_2}\big) \]	Opt evolutionary recombination
What is one-point crossover for bit strings?	The first k bits come from one parent and the remaining bits from the other, with k chosen as the crossover point.	Opt evolutionary recombination
What is mutation in an evolutionary algorithm?	A random perturbation of a candidate, for example uniform or Gaussian noise for real values, or flipping each bit with some probability for binary encodings.	Opt evolutionary mutation
What are the pros and cons of evolutionary algorithms?	They are simple and highly parallelizable, but balancing exploration and exploitation is hard, they have many parameters, and they are unsuited to expensive objective functions.	Opt evolutionary
What is CMA-ES?	An estimation-of-distribution algorithm that samples offspring from a multivariate normal and updates its mean, covariance matrix and overall step size from the best performers.	Opt evolutionary cma-es
What do the mean, covariance and sigma represent in CMA-ES?	The mean is the weighted average of the best solutions, the covariance captures the shape and orientation of the promising region, and sigma is the overall step size.	Opt evolutionary cma-es
What is Bayesian optimization designed for?	Black-box functions with no gradient or analytic description whose evaluation is expensive, so the number of evaluations must be minimized.	Opt bayesian-optimization
Why are naive approaches inadequate for expensive black-box optimization?	Grid search wastes evaluations when variables are correlated or irrelevant, random search is inefficient in higher dimensions, and derivative-free methods like Nelder-Mead need too many evaluations.	Opt bayesian-optimization
What are the four steps of the Bayesian optimization loop?	Create an initial design, fit a surrogate model to the observations, optimize an acquisition function to pick the next point, and evaluate the true function there.	Opt bayesian-optimization
What is Latin hypercube sampling?	An initial design method partitioning each axis into equal-probability bins and placing exactly one sample per bin on every axis, giving good coverage.	Opt bayesian-optimization design
What is maximin sampling?	An initial design maximizing the minimum distance between any two points, which spreads the design out as much as possible for maximal exploration.	Opt bayesian-optimization design
Why is a surrogate model needed in Bayesian optimization?	Because the true function is unknown and expensive, so a cheap approximation is optimized instead to decide where to spend the next expensive evaluation.	Opt bayesian-optimization surrogate
Why is a Gaussian process the standard surrogate model?	Because it is smooth, gives good uncertainty estimates, and its posterior predictive distribution is Gaussian, which makes acquisition functions analytically tractable.	Opt bayesian-optimization gaussian-process
What are the drawbacks of a Gaussian process surrogate?	It scales cubically in the number of data points, handles only numeric inputs, and can suffer numerical problems.	Opt bayesian-optimization gaussian-process
What are the pros and cons of a random forest surrogate?	It is cheap to train, scales well and is robust, but its uncertainty estimates are weak and it is not truly Bayesian since it has no real posterior predictive.	Opt bayesian-optimization surrogate
What is an acquisition function?	A cheap function balancing the surrogate's predicted mean against its predicted uncertainty, used to choose where to evaluate next.	Opt bayesian-optimization acquisition
What is the lower confidence bound acquisition function?	The posterior mean minus a multiple of the posterior standard deviation. The multiplier directly controls the exploration-exploitation trade-off.<br><br>\[ a_{\mathrm{LCB}}(x)=\hat\mu(x)-\kappa\,\hat\sigma(x) \]	Opt bayesian-optimization acquisition
What is the probability of improvement acquisition function?	The probability that the value at a candidate point falls below the best observed value so far, computed from the Gaussian posterior.<br><br>\[ a_{\mathrm{PI}}(x)=\Phi\!\left(\frac{f_{\min}-\hat\mu(x)}{\hat\sigma(x)}\right) \]	Opt bayesian-optimization acquisition
What is the flaw of probability of improvement?	It ignores the size of the improvement, so it tends to propose points very close to the current best rather than exploring.	Opt bayesian-optimization acquisition
What is expected improvement?	The expected magnitude of improvement over the current best, combining how much better a point might be with how likely that is. It rewards both good means and high variance.<br><br>\[ a_{\mathrm{EI}}(x)=E\big[\max\big(f_{\min}-f(x),\ 0\big)\big] \]	Opt bayesian-optimization acquisition
Why is expected improvement a global optimizer?	Because its uncertainty term keeps it non-zero in unexplored regions, so the search cannot permanently ignore any part of the space.	Opt bayesian-optimization acquisition
What is the value of any acquisition function at an already evaluated design point?	Zero, because the posterior standard deviation there is zero and no improvement is possible at a known point.	Opt bayesian-optimization acquisition
What is multi-criteria optimization?	Minimizing several objective functions simultaneously, where improving one typically degrades another.	Opt multi-criteria
What does it mean for one solution to Pareto-dominate another?	It is at least as good in every objective and strictly better in at least one.<br><br>\[ f_i(x)\le f_i(y)\ \forall i\quad\text{and}\quad \exists j:\ f_j(x)\lt f_j(y) \]	Opt multi-criteria pareto
What is the Pareto set and the Pareto front?	The Pareto set is the collection of all non-dominated solutions in the input space; the Pareto front is their image in the objective space.	Opt multi-criteria pareto
What is the difference between a priori and a posteriori approaches?	A priori builds preferences into the problem from the start, typically via weights; a posteriori computes the Pareto set first and lets the decision maker choose afterwards.	Opt multi-criteria
What is the weighted sum method and what does it guarantee?	Collapsing the objectives into one weighted sum. Any minimizer with strictly positive weights is Pareto optimal.<br><br>\[ \min_x\ \sum_{k}w_k\,f_k(x),\qquad w_k\gt 0,\ \textstyle\sum_k w_k=1 \]	Opt multi-criteria scalarization
What is the limitation of the weighted sum method?	The converse fails: only Pareto optimal points on a convex part of the front can be found this way. Concave regions of the front are unreachable.	Opt multi-criteria scalarization
What is proper Pareto optimality?	Pareto optimality with a bounded trade-off ratio, which excludes solutions where a tiny gain in one objective costs a disproportionately large loss in another.	Opt multi-criteria pareto
What is the lexicographic method?	Optimizing the objectives in a fixed priority order: minimize the first, then among all its optima minimize the second, and so on. Different orders produce different solutions.	Opt multi-criteria
What is NSGA-II?	A multi-objective evolutionary algorithm using non-dominated sorting into layers as the main selection criterion and crowding distance as the tie-break.	Opt multi-criteria nsga-ii
What is non-dominated sorting?	Repeatedly extracting the current Pareto front and removing it, which partitions the population into ranked layers of decreasing quality.	Opt multi-criteria nsga-ii
What is crowding distance and what is it for?	The average distance to a point's neighbours in each objective. It is used as a tie-break to favour spread-out solutions over clustered ones, preserving diversity along the front.	Opt multi-criteria nsga-ii
What is the SMS selection strategy?	Excluding the point whose removal costs the least hypervolume, so selection directly optimizes the dominated volume of the front.	Opt multi-criteria hypervolume
What is ParEGO?	Multi-objective Bayesian optimization that collapses the objectives into a single scalar using a randomly drawn weight vector at each iteration, typically via the Chebyshev norm.	Opt multi-criteria bayesian-optimization
What is the problem with hypervolume-based acquisition functions?	Large regions contribute zero hypervolume improvement, so the acquisition surface is flat and uninformative over much of the space.	Opt multi-criteria hypervolume
For which step sizes does gradient descent converge on a positive definite quadratic?	For step sizes strictly between zero and the reciprocal of the largest eigenvalue. Transforming to the eigenbasis shows each coordinate contracts only under that bound.<br><br>\[ 0\ \lt\ \alpha\ \lt\ \frac{2}{\lambda_{\max}} \]	Opt convergence quadratic
When are gradient descent and Newton-Raphson equivalent?	For a quadratic function whose Hessian is a multiple of the identity, when the gradient descent step size is the reciprocal of that multiple.	Opt second-order
Why do second-order methods scale the gradient by the inverse Hessian?	Because the gradient alone does not point at the minimum, is blind to curvature, and is sensitive to the scaling of the variables. The inverse Hessian corrects for all three.	Opt second-order
Why is the empirical risk of logistic regression convex?	Because its second derivative is a sum of strictly positive terms, so the Hessian is positive definite.	Opt logistic-regression convexity
What happens to logistic regression under perfect separation?	The coefficients diverge to infinity, because the likelihood keeps improving as the decision boundary is made steeper without bound.	Opt logistic-regression
What is the QR approach to solving the normal equations?	Decompose the design matrix into an orthogonal and an upper triangular factor, then solve the resulting triangular system by back substitution. It avoids forming the ill-conditioned cross-product matrix.<br><br>\[ X=QR\ \Rightarrow\ R\hat\beta=Q^{\top}y \]	Opt linear-algebra qr
What is a box constraint?	A constraint bounding each variable between a lower and an upper limit, as in the soft margin SVM dual where the dual coefficients lie between zero and C.<br><br>\[ 0\ \le\ \alpha_i\ \le\ C \]	Opt constrained
