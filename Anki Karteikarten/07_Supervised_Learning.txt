What is the true risk of a model?	The expected loss over the joint distribution of features and target. It is what we actually want to minimize but cannot compute, since the distribution is unknown.<br><br>\[ \mathcal{R}(f)=E_{(x,y)\sim P}\big[L\big(y,f(x)\big)\big] \]	SL risk theory
What is the empirical risk?	The average loss over the observed training data. It is the computable surrogate for the true risk.<br><br>\[ \mathcal{R}_{\text{emp}}(f)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}L\big(y_i,f(x_i)\big) \]	SL risk theory
What is the optimal constant model?	The single constant value minimizing expected loss, ignoring all features. It is the baseline any real model must beat.	SL risk baseline
What is the Bayes optimal model?	The model minimizing true risk over all possible functions. It is the theoretically best achievable predictor and its risk is the Bayes risk.<br><br>\[ f^{*}(x)=\arg\min_{c}\ E\big[L(y,c)\mid x\big] \]	SL risk bayes-optimal
Why is the Bayes optimal model pointwise optimal?	Because the risk can be written as an expectation over x of the conditional expected loss, so minimizing the inner conditional loss separately at each x is optimal.	SL risk bayes-optimal
What is Bayes regret and how does it decompose?	The excess risk of the fitted model over the Bayes risk. It splits into estimation error (from finite data) plus approximation error (from a restricted hypothesis space).<br><br>\[ \mathcal{R}(\hat f)-\mathcal{R}(f^*)=\underbrace{\mathcal{R}(\hat f)-\mathcal{R}(f_{\mathcal{H}})}_{\text{estimation}}+\underbrace{\mathcal{R}(f_{\mathcal{H}})-\mathcal{R}(f^*)}_{\text{approximation}} \]	SL risk decomposition
What is approximation error?	The gap between the best model in the chosen hypothesis space and the Bayes optimal model. It comes from restricting the model class.	SL risk decomposition
What is estimation error?	The gap between the model actually fitted on finite data and the best possible model in the hypothesis space. It comes from having limited data.	SL risk decomposition
What is optimization error?	The gap between the local optimum the training algorithm actually reached and the global optimum on the training data.	SL risk decomposition
How do the three error sources map onto bias and variance?	Bias comes from the approximation error plus estimation bias; variance comes from the optimization error plus estimation variance.	SL risk bias-variance
What is a residual in regression? (Supervised Learning)	The difference between the observed target and the prediction. Regression losses are functions of the residual.	SL loss-functions
What is the margin in classification?	The product of the true label (coded as plus or minus 1) and the model score. It is positive for correct predictions and its magnitude reflects confidence.<br><br>\[ m=y\cdot f(x),\qquad y\in\{-1,+1\} \]	SL loss-functions margin
What is a symmetric loss function?	One that is unchanged when the true value and the prediction are swapped. Over- and under-prediction are penalized identically.	SL loss-functions
What is a translation invariant loss?	One that does not change when the same constant is added to both the true value and the prediction. Only the residual matters, not the absolute level.	SL loss-functions
What are pseudo-residuals?	The negative derivative of the loss with respect to the prediction. They generalize residuals to arbitrary losses and drive gradient boosting.<br><br>\[ \tilde r_i=-\left.\frac{\partial L\big(y_i,f\big)}{\partial f}\right|_{f=\hat f(x_i)} \]	SL loss-functions boosting
What is the L2 loss and what is its risk minimizer?	The squared residual. Its theoretical risk minimizer is the conditional expectation of y given x, and its empirical minimizer is the sample mean.<br><br>\[ L(y,f)=(y-f)^2,\qquad f^*(x)=E(y\mid x) \]	SL loss-functions l2
What is the L1 loss and what is its risk minimizer?	The absolute residual. Its risk minimizer is the conditional median, which makes it robust to outliers.<br><br>\[ L(y,f)=|y-f|,\qquad f^*(x)=\mathrm{median}(y\mid x) \]	SL loss-functions l1
What is the drawback of L1 loss for optimization?	It is not differentiable at zero, which complicates gradient-based methods.	SL loss-functions l1
What is the Huber loss?	A loss that is quadratic for small residuals and linear for large ones. It is everywhere differentiable and robust to outliers at the same time.<br><br>\[ L=\begin{cases}\tfrac{1}{2}r^2,&amp; |r|\le\delta\\ \delta\big(|r|-\tfrac{\delta}{2}\big),&amp; |r|\gt \delta\end{cases} \]	SL loss-functions huber
What is the epsilon-insensitive loss?	A loss that is exactly zero for residuals within a tolerance band and linear outside it. It ignores small errors entirely and yields sparse solutions.<br><br>\[ L=\max\big(0,\ |r|-\epsilon\big) \]	SL loss-functions epsilon-insensitive
What is the quantile loss and what does it estimate?	An asymmetric absolute loss weighting over- and under-prediction differently. Its risk minimizer is the corresponding conditional quantile.<br><br>\[ L_\tau(r)=\begin{cases}\tau\,r,&amp; r\ge 0\\ (\tau-1)\,r,&amp; r\lt 0\end{cases} \]	SL loss-functions quantile
What is the 0-1 loss?	A loss of 1 for a wrong class prediction and 0 for a correct one. Its risk is simply the misclassification probability.<br><br>\[ L(y,\hat y)=\mathbf{1}\{y\neq\hat y\} \]	SL loss-functions 0-1
What is the Bayes optimal model for the 0-1 loss?	The most probable class given x. The corresponding Bayes risk is one minus the expected maximum class probability.	SL loss-functions 0-1
What is the optimal constant model for 0-1 loss?	The most frequent class in the data, that is, the mode of the labels.	SL loss-functions 0-1
What is the Bernoulli (log) loss?	The negative log-likelihood of a Bernoulli variable, penalizing the log of the predicted probability of the true class. Its risk minimizer is the true conditional probability.<br><br>\[ L=-\big[y\log\pi+(1-y)\log(1-\pi)\big] \]	SL loss-functions log-loss
Why does log loss penalize confident wrong predictions so heavily?	Because the logarithm goes to minus infinity as the predicted probability of the true class approaches zero. Extreme overconfidence is punished without bound.	SL loss-functions log-loss
What is a proper scoring rule?	A loss on predicted probability distributions whose expected value is minimized by the true distribution. It rewards honest probability reporting.	SL loss-functions scoring-rules
Why is the L1 loss not a proper scoring rule for probabilities?	Because its expected value is minimized at 0 or 1 rather than at the true probability. It pushes toward extreme predictions.	SL loss-functions scoring-rules
Is the 0-1 loss a proper scoring rule?	It is proper but not strictly proper: any predicted probability on the correct side of the threshold gives the same expected loss, so it does not identify the true probability.	SL loss-functions scoring-rules
What is the Brier score?	The squared difference between the predicted probability and the true label indicator: the L2 loss applied to classification. It is a strictly proper scoring rule.<br><br>\[ \mathrm{BS}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(\hat\pi_i-y_i\big)^2 \]	SL loss-functions brier
What is calibration of predicted probabilities?	The property that among all cases predicted with probability p, a fraction p really are positive. Predicted probabilities match observed frequencies.	SL evaluation calibration
What is the hinge loss?	The maximum of zero and one minus the margin. It is zero once a point is correctly classified with sufficient margin.<br><br>\[ L=\max\big(0,\ 1-y\,f(x)\big) \]	SL loss-functions hinge
What does the hinge loss encourage?	Confident correct predictions: it keeps penalizing until the margin exceeds 1, not merely until the sign is correct.	SL loss-functions hinge
How does squared hinge differ from ordinary hinge loss?	It squares the violation, making it more sensitive to outliers but differentiable everywhere.	SL loss-functions hinge
What is the exponential loss?	The exponential of the negative margin. It is never exactly zero and grows extremely fast for misclassified points, which is why AdaBoost is outlier-sensitive.<br><br>\[ L=\exp\big(-y\,f(x)\big) \]	SL loss-functions exponential
What is the connection between maximum likelihood and empirical risk minimization?	Defining the negative log-likelihood as the loss turns maximum likelihood into empirical risk minimization. Every distributional assumption induces a loss.	SL theory likelihood
Which distributions correspond to L2, L1 and log loss?	Gaussian errors give L2 loss, Laplace errors give L1 loss, and a Bernoulli response gives the log loss.	SL theory likelihood
Does every loss correspond to a distribution?	No. Every distribution induces a loss, but not every loss arises from a probability distribution.	SL theory likelihood
What is generalization error?	The expected loss on unseen test data. It is what actually matters, as opposed to training error.	SL theory generalization
What are the three components of the generalization error decomposition?	The irreducible noise variance, the variance of the learner at that point, and the squared bias of the learner.<br><br>\[ E\big[(y-\hat f)^2\big]=\sigma^2+\operatorname{Var}\big(\hat f\big)+\mathrm{Bias}\big(\hat f\big)^2 \]	SL theory bias-variance
What is the variance of a learner?	How much the fitted model deviates from the average model across different training sets. High variance means instability with respect to the data.	SL theory bias-variance
What is the bias of a learner?	The difference between the average fitted model and the true underlying model. High bias means systematic misspecification.	SL theory bias-variance
How is multiclass classification risk formulated?	As an expectation over x of the sum, over all classes, of the loss weighted by the conditional class probability.	SL classification multiclass
What is the difference between scores, probabilities and hard labels?	Scores are unconstrained real outputs per class, probabilities are normalized scores summing to 1, and hard labels are the single chosen class.	SL classification multiclass
What is softmax regression?	Multiclass logistic regression: one linear score per class, transformed into probabilities by the softmax function, trained with the log loss.<br><br>\[ \pi_k(x)=\frac{\exp\big(x^{\top}\beta_k\big)}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}\exp\big(x^{\top}\beta_j\big)} \]	SL classification softmax
What is surprisal in information theory?	The negative logarithm of a probability. Rare events carry high surprisal and common events carry little.<br><br>\[ I(x)=-\log p(x) \]	SL information-theory
What is entropy?	The expected surprisal of a random variable: how hard the outcome is to predict on average. It is always non-negative for discrete variables.<br><br>\[ H(X)=-\sum_x p(x)\log p(x) \]	SL information-theory entropy
For which discrete distribution is entropy maximal?	The uniform distribution. When all outcomes are equally likely there is the most uncertainty about the result.	SL information-theory entropy
What is differential entropy and how does it differ from discrete entropy?	The continuous analogue using a density instead of probabilities. Unlike discrete entropy it can be negative.	SL information-theory entropy
Which continuous distribution maximizes entropy for a fixed variance?	The normal distribution. Among all distributions with a given variance the Gaussian is the most uncertain.	SL information-theory entropy
How does differential entropy behave under shifting and scaling?	Shifting leaves it unchanged, while scaling by a factor adds the log of the absolute scaling factor. Entropy is location invariant but not scale invariant.	SL information-theory entropy
What is joint entropy?	The entropy of a pair of random variables considered together. For independent variables it is the sum of the individual entropies.<br><br>\[ H(X,Y)=-\sum_{x,y}p(x,y)\log p(x,y) \]	SL information-theory entropy
What is conditional entropy?	The remaining uncertainty about one variable once the other is known. It is at most the unconditional entropy, since information cannot hurt.<br><br>\[ H(Y\mid X)=H(X,Y)-H(X)\ \le\ H(Y) \]	SL information-theory entropy
What is the chain rule of entropy?	The joint entropy equals the entropy of one variable plus the conditional entropy of the other given it.<br><br>\[ H(X,Y)=H(X)+H(Y\mid X) \]	SL information-theory entropy
What is the Kullback-Leibler divergence?	The expected log-likelihood ratio between two distributions with the same support. It measures how far an approximation is from the original.<br><br>\[ \mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,q)=\sum_x p(x)\log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\ \ge\ 0 \]	SL information-theory kl-divergence
What are the two key properties of KL divergence?	It is always non-negative and equals zero only when the distributions coincide, but it is not symmetric, so it is not a proper distance.	SL information-theory kl-divergence
What is cross-entropy conceptually?	How hard it is to predict outcomes from p when you encode them using q. It equals the entropy of p plus the KL divergence from p to q.<br><br>\[ H(p,q)=-\sum_x p(x)\log q(x)=H(p)+\mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,q) \]	SL information-theory cross-entropy
What is the relationship between minimizing KL divergence and maximum likelihood?	They are equivalent. Minimizing the KL divergence from the data distribution to the model is the same as maximizing the expected log-likelihood.	SL information-theory kl-divergence
What is mutual information?	The information about one variable gained by knowing the other, defined as the KL divergence between the joint distribution and the product of the marginals.<br><br>\[ I(X;Y)=\mathrm{KL}\big(p(x,y)\,\|\,p(x)p(y)\big)=H(X)-H(X\mid Y) \]	SL information-theory mutual-information
How is mutual information expressed in terms of entropies?	As the entropy of one variable minus its conditional entropy given the other. It is symmetric and zero exactly when the variables are independent.	SL information-theory mutual-information
What is the maximum entropy principle?	When only partial information is known, choose the distribution with the highest entropy consistent with it. This avoids assuming anything not actually given.	SL information-theory maximum-entropy
What is source coding and what does entropy have to do with it?	Assigning shorter codewords to more frequent symbols. The entropy of the source is the optimal achievable average codeword length.	SL information-theory source-coding
What is a prefix code?	A code in which no codeword is a prefix of another, so the stream can be decoded unambiguously without separators.	SL information-theory source-coding
What is the coding interpretation of cross-entropy?	The average codeword length obtained when encoding data from p with a code optimized for q. The excess over the entropy is exactly the KL divergence.	SL information-theory cross-entropy
What is the curse of dimensionality regarding volume?	In high dimensions almost all the volume of a region lies in a thin outer shell, so uniformly distributed points end up near the boundary.	SL theory curse-of-dimensionality
What happens to distances in high dimensions?	The maximum and minimum pairwise distances converge toward each other, so distance-based notions of similarity lose their discriminative power.	SL theory curse-of-dimensionality
Why does high dimension make data sparse?	Because the number of distinguishable regions grows exponentially with dimension, so no realistic sample size can populate them all.	SL theory curse-of-dimensionality
What are the three remedies for the curse of dimensionality?	Dimension reduction such as PCA or feature selection, collecting more data, and regularization.	SL theory curse-of-dimensionality
What is the difference between explicit and implicit regularization?	Explicit regularization adds a penalty term for model complexity; implicit regularization arises from procedures like early stopping, ensembling or dropout.	SL regularization
What is regularized risk?	The empirical risk plus a complexity penalty scaled by a control parameter that trades off fit against simplicity.<br><br>\[ \mathcal{R}_{\text{reg}}(\theta)=\mathcal{R}_{\text{emp}}(\theta)+\lambda\,J(\theta) \]	SL regularization
Why is the intercept normally excluded from the regularization penalty?	Because penalizing it would shrink the model toward zero rather than toward a constant, which is not what regularization is meant to do.	SL regularization
Why does regularization require standardized features?	Because the penalty depends on coefficient size, and downscaling a feature inflates its coefficient, which would make it appear more heavily penalized.	SL regularization standardization
What is ridge regression?	Least squares with an L2 penalty. The closed-form solution adds a multiple of the identity to the cross-product matrix before inverting.<br><br>\[ \hat\beta=\big(X^{\top}X+\lambda I\big)^{-1}X^{\top}y \]	SL regularization ridge
Why does ridge regularization help when the design matrix is not full rank?	Because adding a positive multiple of the identity makes the matrix invertible, so a unique solution exists even with collinear features.	SL regularization ridge
What is lasso regression?	Least squares with an L1 penalty. It can shrink coefficients exactly to zero, so it performs variable selection.<br><br>\[ \hat\beta=\arg\min_\beta\ \|y-X\beta\|_2^2+\lambda\|\beta\|_1 \]	SL regularization lasso
Why does lasso produce exact zeros while ridge does not?	Because the L1 constraint region has corners on the axes, so the optimum frequently lands exactly on an axis where a coefficient is zero.	SL regularization lasso
What does lasso do with two highly correlated features?	It typically selects one and excludes the other, rather than splitting the coefficient between them.	SL regularization lasso
What is elastic net?	A combination of L1 and L2 penalties. One parameter controls overall regularization strength and another controls the balance between the two penalty types.<br><br>\[ J(\beta)=\lambda\Big(\alpha\|\beta\|_1+\tfrac{1-\alpha}{2}\|\beta\|_2^2\Big) \]	SL regularization elastic-net
What is L0 regularization and where does it appear?	A penalty on the raw count of non-zero features. It is the principle behind information criteria such as AIC and BIC.<br><br>\[ J(\beta)=\|\beta\|_0=\#\{j:\beta_j\neq 0\} \]	SL regularization l0
What is the minimax concave penalty (MCP)?	A penalty that behaves like L1 for small coefficients but flattens to a constant for large ones. This produces less bias than L1 or L2 for large true effects.	SL regularization mcp
What is the geometric interpretation of L2 regularization in eigen-coordinates?	Each eigen-direction of the Hessian is shrunk by a factor of its eigenvalue over that eigenvalue plus the penalty. Flat directions shrink most.<br><br>\[ \tilde\beta_i=\frac{\lambda_i}{\lambda_i+\lambda}\,\hat\beta_i \]	SL regularization l2 geometry
What is the closed-form relationship between the ridge and unregularized solutions?	The ridge solution equals the Hessian plus the penalty times the identity, inverted, times the Hessian times the unregularized solution.	SL regularization ridge
Why is the first-order term absent in the Taylor expansion of the risk at the minimizer?	Because the gradient vanishes at a minimum, so the expansion starts with the quadratic Hessian term.	SL regularization taylor
What is L2 regularization interpreted as weight decay?	The gradient step multiplies the old weights by one minus the learning rate times the penalty before applying the usual gradient. Weights shrink at every step.<br><br>\[ \theta^{(t+1)}=(1-\alpha\lambda)\,\theta^{(t)}-\alpha\,\nabla_\theta\mathcal{R}_{\text{emp}} \]	SL regularization weight-decay
What is structural risk minimization? (Supervised Learning)	Considering a nested sequence of hypothesis spaces of increasing size and choosing the smallest one that is not significantly outperformed by a larger one.	SL theory model-selection
What is the Bayesian interpretation of regularization?	The penalty is the negative log of a prior on the parameters. Maximizing the posterior corresponds to minimizing loss plus penalty.	SL regularization bayes
Which priors correspond to L2 and L1 regularization?	A Gaussian prior gives L2 regularization, a Laplace prior gives L1, and a flat prior gives no regularization at all.<br><br>\[ \text{Gauss prior}\ \Rightarrow\ L_2,\qquad \text{Laplace prior}\ \Rightarrow\ L_1 \]	SL regularization bayes
What is the margin of a linear classifier?	The smallest distance from any training point to the separating hyperplane. Maximizing it is the core idea of the SVM.<br><br>\[ \gamma=\min_i\ \frac{y_i\big(w^{\top}x_i+b\big)}{\|w\|} \]	SL svm margin
What is the hard margin linear SVM problem?	Find the separating hyperplane that maximizes the margin, subject to every point being correctly classified with margin at least 1 after normalization.<br><br>\[ \min_{w,b}\ \tfrac{1}{2}\|w\|^2\quad\text{s.t.}\quad y_i\big(w^{\top}x_i+b\big)\ge 1 \]	SL svm
Why is the SVM objective written as minimizing the squared norm of the weights?	Because the margin is the reciprocal of the weight norm, so maximizing the margin is the same as minimizing that norm. The normalization also makes the solution unique.	SL svm
What is a support vector?	A training point lying exactly on the margin boundary or violating it. Only these points have non-zero dual coefficients and determine the hyperplane.	SL svm support-vectors
What is the dual SVM problem and why is it useful?	A maximization over one coefficient per training point, in which the data appear only through inner products. This is what makes the kernel trick possible.<br><br>\[ \max_\alpha\ \sum_i\alpha_i-\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\alpha_i\alpha_jy_iy_j\,\langle x_i,x_j\rangle \]	SL svm duality
What is the soft margin SVM?	An SVM allowing margin violations through slack variables, with a cost parameter C penalizing the total violation.<br><br>\[ \min_{w,b,\xi}\ \tfrac{1}{2}\|w\|^2+C\sum_i\xi_i\quad\text{s.t.}\quad y_i\big(w^{\top}x_i+b\big)\ge 1-\xi_i,\ \xi_i\ge 0 \]	SL svm soft-margin
What does the cost parameter C control in a soft margin SVM?	The trade-off between margin width and violations. A large C makes violations expensive and prioritizes correct classification over a wide margin.	SL svm soft-margin
How do the dual coefficients classify training points in a soft margin SVM?	A coefficient of zero means a non-support vector, a value strictly between zero and C means a support vector exactly on the margin, and a value equal to C means a margin violator.	SL svm support-vectors
How can the SVM be seen as empirical risk minimization?	The primal problem is exactly L2-regularized empirical risk minimization with the hinge loss.<br><br>\[ \min_{w}\ \sum_i\max\big(0,1-y_if(x_i)\big)+\frac{1}{2C}\|w\|^2 \]	SL svm erm
What is the kernel trick?	Replacing every inner product in the dual problem by a kernel function, so the feature transformation never has to be computed explicitly.<br><br>\[ \langle x_i,x_j\rangle\ \longrightarrow\ k(x_i,x_j)=\big\langle\phi(x_i),\phi(x_j)\big\rangle \]	SL svm kernel
What is a valid kernel?	A symmetric function whose Gram matrix is positive semi-definite for every finite set of inputs. These two properties are what have to be checked.	SL svm kernel
What operations preserve kernels?	Multiplying by a non-negative constant, adding two kernels, and multiplying two kernels all yield kernels again.	SL svm kernel
What is the Gaussian (RBF) kernel and what does it do?	A kernel decaying exponentially with squared distance, creating localized bumps around each support vector. Its width parameter controls how wiggly the boundary is.<br><br>\[ k(x,x')=\exp\!\left(-\frac{\|x-x'\|^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) \]	SL svm kernel rbf
What is the difference between homogeneous and non-homogeneous polynomial kernels?	The homogeneous version is the plain inner product raised to a power; the non-homogeneous version adds a constant before exponentiating, which also includes lower-order terms.<br><br>\[ k(x,x')=\big(x^{\top}x'\big)^{d}\qquad\text{vs.}\qquad \big(x^{\top}x'+c\big)^{d} \]	SL svm kernel
What does Mercer's theorem state?	For every valid kernel there exists a Hilbert space in which the kernel acts as an inner product. The feature map is guaranteed to exist without ever being constructed.	SL svm kernel mercer
What is the reproducing property?	Evaluating a function in the space against the kernel centered at a point returns the function value at that point. The kernel reproduces evaluation via an inner product.<br><br>\[ \big\langle f,\ k(\cdot,x)\big\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=f(x) \]	SL svm kernel rkhs
What does the representer theorem state?	The optimal solution can be written as a linear combination of the kernel evaluated at the training points. The infinite-dimensional problem collapses to n coefficients.<br><br>\[ \hat f(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_i\,k(x_i,x) \]	SL svm representer-theorem
Are SVMs parametric or non-parametric?	Non-parametric: the number of effective parameters is determined by the data through the support vectors rather than fixed in advance.	SL svm
How can sensible SVM hyperparameters be found?	By computing quantiles of the pairwise distances between training points, which gives the relevant length scale for the kernel width.	SL svm hyperparameters
What is universal consistency for SVMs?	With a universal kernel, the SVM risk converges to the Bayes risk as the sample size grows. It can approach optimality in the limit.	SL svm theory
What is boosting?	Sequentially fitting weak base learners, each focusing on what the previous ones got wrong, and combining them into a strong weighted ensemble.	SL boosting
What is the core idea of AdaBoost?	Reweight the training data after each round so that misclassified points get more weight, and weight each base learner by how accurate it was.	SL boosting adaboost
How is the AdaBoost base learner weight computed?	As one half the log of the ratio of correct to incorrect weighted error. Accurate learners get large positive weights and near-random ones get almost zero.<br><br>\[ \alpha_m=\tfrac{1}{2}\log\frac{1-\mathrm{err}_m}{\mathrm{err}_m} \]	SL boosting adaboost
How are AdaBoost observation weights updated?	Multiplied by the exponential of the negative learner weight times the margin, then renormalized to sum to 1. Misclassified points gain weight.<br><br>\[ w_i^{(m+1)}\propto w_i^{(m)}\exp\big(-\alpha_m\,y_i\,h_m(x_i)\big) \]	SL boosting adaboost
What is the tuning parameter of AdaBoost?	The number of boosting iterations. Too many can eventually overfit.	SL boosting adaboost
What is forward stagewise additive modelling?	Building an additive model one term at a time, at each step fitting the new base learner and its coefficient while keeping all previous terms fixed.<br><br>\[ \hat f^{(m)}(x)=\hat f^{(m-1)}(x)+\beta_m\,b\big(x;\theta_m\big) \]	SL boosting additive-model
Why is the model built one component at a time?	Because jointly optimizing all base learners is a very high-dimensional problem. Greedy stagewise fitting makes it tractable.	SL boosting additive-model
What is gradient boosting?	Boosting interpreted as gradient descent in function space: each new base learner is fitted to the pseudo-residuals, which are the negative gradients of the loss.	SL boosting gradient-boosting
Why is the base learner fitted to the pseudo-residuals rather than used directly as the gradient?	Because the raw negative gradient is only defined at the training points, which would just memorize the data. Fitting a base learner to it generalizes to new points.	SL boosting gradient-boosting
What are the pseudo-residuals for the L2 loss?	The ordinary residuals. For the L1 loss they are the sign of the residual instead.<br><br>\[ \tilde r_i=y_i-\hat f(x_i)\qquad\text{(L1: }\ \mathrm{sign}\big(y_i-\hat f(x_i)\big)) \]	SL boosting gradient-boosting
What are the three main hyperparameters of boosted trees?	The number of base learners, the learning rate, and the maximum tree depth.	SL boosting hyperparameters
What is stochastic gradient boosting?	Fitting each base learner on a random subsample of the data rather than the full set. The added randomness acts as regularization.	SL boosting regularization
How does gradient boosting handle multiclass classification?	One score function per class, with softmax probabilities computed at each iteration and a separate base learner fitted per class to the difference between the label indicator and the predicted probability.	SL boosting multiclass
How is a tree written as an additive base learner?	As a sum over terminal regions of a constant times the indicator that the observation falls in that region. Trees are piecewise-constant functions.<br><br>\[ b(x)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}c_t\,\mathbf{1}\{x\in R_t\} \]	SL trees boosting
What three extra regularization terms does XGBoost add?	A penalty on the number of leaves, an L2 penalty on the leaf values, and an L1 penalty on the leaf values.	SL boosting xgboost
What does XGBoost's random dropping of previous base learners achieve?	It interpolates between methods: dropping none is ordinary gradient boosting, and dropping all of them corresponds to a random forest.	SL boosting xgboost
What is componentwise gradient boosting?	Boosting where at each step several candidate base learners, typically one per feature, are fitted and only the best one is added. This gives automatic feature selection.	SL boosting componentwise
Why are additive base learners required in componentwise boosting?	So that two base learners of the same type can be merged by adding their parameters, keeping the final model interpretable as one term per feature.	SL boosting componentwise
What is the relation between componentwise boosting and GLMs?	Specifying the negative log-likelihood of an exponential family distribution as the loss makes componentwise boosting equivalent to fitting a GLM.	SL boosting glm
What is the relation between componentwise boosting and GAMs?	Using penalized splines as base learners makes componentwise boosting equivalent to fitting a generalized additive model.	SL boosting gam
What is non-linear effect decomposition in boosting?	Splitting each base learner into a constant, a linear, and a non-linear part, so the model reveals how much of each feature's effect is genuinely non-linear.	SL boosting interpretability
What is the Bayesian linear model?	A linear model with a Gaussian likelihood and a Gaussian prior on the coefficients, which yields a Gaussian posterior in closed form.	SL bayes linear-model
What is a Gaussian process?	A distribution over functions such that any finite set of function values has a joint multivariate normal distribution, specified by a mean function and a covariance kernel.<br><br>\[ f(\cdot)\sim\mathcal{GP}\big(m(\cdot),\,k(\cdot,\cdot)\big) \]	SL gaussian-process
What are the three common kinds of GP kernels?	Stationary kernels depending on the difference of inputs, isotropic kernels depending only on the distance, and dot product kernels depending on the inner product.	SL gaussian-process kernel
What is the posterior predictive distribution of a Gaussian process?	Again Gaussian, with a mean that is a kernel-weighted combination of the observed targets and a variance reduced by what the training data already explain.<br><br>\[ \mu_*=k_*^{\top}\big(K+\sigma^2I\big)^{-1}y,\qquad \sigma_*^2=k_{**}-k_*^{\top}\big(K+\sigma^2I\big)^{-1}k_* \]	SL gaussian-process prediction
What is the key advantage of a Gaussian process over a point predictor?	It returns a full predictive distribution, so uncertainty is quantified and grows naturally in regions far from the training data.	SL gaussian-process
How is observation noise handled in a Gaussian process?	By adding the noise variance to the diagonal of the kernel matrix. The GP then interpolates the underlying function rather than the noisy observations.<br><br>\[ K\ \longrightarrow\ K+\sigma_n^2 I \]	SL gaussian-process noise
